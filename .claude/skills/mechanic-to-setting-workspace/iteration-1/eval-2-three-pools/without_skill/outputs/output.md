# Three-Pool Resource Management: Setting and Narrative Concepts

## The Core Mechanic, Interpreted

You have three pools that drain, can be fed into each other at a cost, and can be replenished by fulfilling orders. The pressure increases over time. The goal is survival. This is fundamentally a triage game — you are always deciding which scarcity matters most right now.

That mechanical skeleton maps naturally onto any setting where three distinct but interdependent systems must all stay alive. The best settings are ones where the three pools feel meaningfully different from each other (not just "red bar, blue bar, green bar") and where the idea of "transferring between them at a cost" has intuitive diegetic logic.

Below are several setting concepts, each with a brief explanation of how the pools, drain, transfer cost, and orders map to that world.

---

## Setting Option 1: A Dying Space Station

**Pools:** Oxygen / Power / Structural Integrity

The station is slowly falling apart. Oxygen leaks, power degrades, hull stress accumulates. Players route resources between systems — but rerouting power to oxygen scrubbers strains the hull, patching the hull draws from power reserves, and so on. Orders are distress calls or supply drops from nearby ships: deliver a working component, seal a specific section, provide breathable air to a docked refugee shuttle.

Why it works: The three pools are intuitive. Everyone understands that a space station needs all three. The transfer cost (doing one thing stresses another) is physically believable. Accelerating drain maps perfectly to cascading system failure — the longer you survive, the more degraded everything becomes. Orders feel urgent and grounded.

Tone options: Hard sci-fi survival, retrofuturist pulp, horror isolation.

---

## Setting Option 2: A Medieval Kingdom in Decline

**Pools:** Food / Morale / Order (law and stability)

The kingdom is past its peak. Harvests shrink, the population grows restless, bandits and rebels fill the power vacuum. Players manage the royal household's diminishing capacity: taxing the peasants raises Order but destroys Morale; executing criminals restores Order but traumatizes the population; feasting boosts Morale but drains Food. Orders are petitions from nobles, guilds, and villages — fulfill them to receive tribute, grain shipments, or pledges of loyalty.

Why it works: The three pools are socially intuitive. The transfer cost has a moral weight — every stabilizing action has a human cost. The accelerating drain is the arc of imperial collapse, which is historically legible. Orders arriving as petitions fits the genre perfectly.

Tone options: Grim realism, dark fantasy, even satirical (running a failing kingdom as dark comedy).

---

## Setting Option 3: A Research Outpost in a Hostile Environment

**Pools:** Data Integrity / Equipment Condition / Researcher Wellbeing

A remote scientific station — Antarctic, deep ocean, alien planet — is slowly degrading. Data corrupts, equipment breaks down, people burn out. Players allocate limited maintenance cycles: repairing equipment consumes researcher time (harming wellbeing), manual data recovery is exhausting and error-prone, keeping morale high means less time on critical repairs. Orders are research directives from the sponsoring organization — complete experiments, submit reports, validate findings — in exchange for resupply shipments.

Why it works: The three pools feel distinct and real. The tension between human welfare and operational necessity is emotionally resonant. Orders as institutional mandates creates a nice irony: the organization demanding results is also the one keeping you alive. Accelerating drain reflects cumulative neglect, which any researcher will recognize.

Tone options: Literary sci-fi, near-future realism, psychological thriller.

---

## Setting Option 4: An Underground City After a Surface Catastrophe

**Pools:** Air Quality / Social Cohesion / Infrastructure

The surface became uninhabitable. The city went underground. Now the filters are aging, factions are forming, and the tunnels are cracking. Players manage the civic council's decisions: expanding living space fractures air filtration networks; cracking down on faction violence restores cohesion but damages infrastructure when fights break out in the walls; prioritizing repairs means neglecting community needs. Orders are contracts with tunnel-runner traders who bring in outside materials in exchange for manufactured goods or services.

Why it works: The three pools represent the three classic survival layers — environment, society, physical infrastructure. Transfer costs feel like policy tradeoffs, which is politically resonant. Orders as trade contracts with outsiders gives a sense of a wider world without requiring the player to engage with it.

Tone options: Solarpunk struggling, dystopian management, hopeful apocalypse.

---

## Setting Option 5: A Long-Haul Cargo Ship

**Pools:** Fuel / Crew Readiness / Cargo Integrity

A commercial freighter on a multi-month journey. Fuel burns, crew tires, cargo shifts and degrades. Players manage the captain's decisions: pushing harder reaches waypoints faster but exhausts crew and burns more fuel; running lean on fuel means slower burns but cargo degrades longer; keeping crew rested delays maintenance. Orders are contracts negotiated at waypoints — deliver specific goods intact, pick up passengers, perform services in exchange for refueling or crew rotation.

Why it works: The setting is mundane and grounded, which makes the escalating pressure feel relatable rather than fantastical. The transfer costs are believable tradeoffs. Orders as commercial contracts fit the blue-collar tone. Accelerating drain maps to the reality that nothing holds together indefinitely on a long voyage.

Tone options: Working-class sci-fi, nautical analog, slice-of-life with existential stakes.

## Cross-Cutting Design Considerations

Regardless of setting, a few design principles apply:

**The three pools should be in tension, not parallel.** If all three drain for the same reason and are replenished by the same actions, the player is just watching three health bars. The mechanic is most interesting when the pools are in systemic conflict — doing well on one pool tends to stress another. The transfer cost mechanic enforces this, but the setting should reinforce it narratively.

**Orders should feel like relief, not just chores.** The best order systems in games like this give the player a moment of exhaling — "finally, I can stabilize the food pool." The orders should clearly name which pool they replenish, so players can prioritize based on their current crisis.

**The accelerating drain should have a narrative explanation.** Unexplained accelerating difficulty feels arbitrary. If it's cascading system failure, political instability compounding, equipment wearing faster because it's already worn — the player feels like they're losing for reasons they understand, which is more engaging than losing to a hidden number going up.

**The cost of transfer should be visible and interpretable.** "You lose 10 fuel to gain 8 crew readiness" is mechanical. "You reroute heating from the cargo hold to crew quarters — the cargo will suffer" is a decision. The setting should give the player a mental model for why transfer has friction.

---

## Recommendation

For a browser-based idle-ish game with no combat and single-player focus, I'd lean toward the **Space Station** or **Underground City** settings. Both have:
- An obvious visual language (gauges, readouts, facility maps)
- Intuitive pool identities that don't require explanation
- A tone that supports the gradual-doom emotional arc of the mechanic
- Clear diegetic logic for why orders exist and why transfer costs something

The **Research Outpost** setting is worth considering if you want a more literary tone and are interested in the ethical dimension (institutional pressure vs. human welfare) as a thematic layer.

The **Medieval Kingdom** is excellent if you want a setting that feels more strategic and less sci-fi, and if you're comfortable with a slightly more abstract visual style.
