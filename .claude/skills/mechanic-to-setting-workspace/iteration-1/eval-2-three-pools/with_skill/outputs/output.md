# Mechanic-to-Setting: Three Draining Pools

## Mechanical Identity

**Core mechanics extracted from your description:**

1. **Core loops** — Moment-to-moment: monitor pool levels → decide whether to transfer or fulfill orders → execute action → watch levels shift. Session loop: resources deplete steadily; player juggles incoming "order" tasks against a worsening drain spiral until one pool hits zero.

2. **Resource dynamics** — Three explicit, parallel resources (the pools). Resources are *consumed passively by time*, not by player action. Transfers convert one resource into another at a loss (the transfer cost is a friction/tax mechanic). Orders are the *only source of net gain* — the player must "sell work" to stay alive.

3. **Tension structure** — Three simultaneous pressures (one per pool) that compound over time (accelerating drain). Transfer cost creates a "robbing Peter to pay Paul" dilemma. Orders create a pull toward specific resources at specific moments — sometimes the order you need conflicts with your most urgent deficit. The three-pool structure means the player always has competing urgencies.

4. **Progression arc** — A pure survival arc. The player starts comfortable and is slowly overwhelmed. Skill expresses as lasting longer (endurance/score-attack) or reaching a higher difficulty threshold before collapse. No unlock progression is described — this reads as a focused, single-loop game.

5. **Interaction model** — Single-player. No adversarial entities; the threat is the system itself (entropy, time, the drain rate).

6. **Perspective and pacing** — Browser-based, idle-ish. Likely a top-down or flat UI view. The pacing is slow-to-medium with periodic urgency spikes — not twitchy, but not fully idle either. The player has time to think but cannot be fully hands-off.

**Mechanical identity statement:** You are a manager maintaining three interdependent systems against inevitable entropy, trading inefficiency for stability, and accepting outside "contracts" to fund the whole operation. The game is about *triage* — choosing which pressure to address now and which to defer.

### 1. Tidal Station

**Elevator Pitch:** You are the keeper of a remote coastal power station, balancing three generators fed by tidal flows — managing fuel reserves, battery banks, and grid pressure to keep the lights on for the town downstream.

**Mechanical Mapping:**
- *Three draining pools* = Three power subsystems: tidal turbine fuel reserve, battery storage charge, and grid pressure (live demand load). Each depletes at a different rate and for different reasons. Turbines drain fuel. Batteries self-discharge. Grid pressure bleeds off as the town draws power.
- *Accelerating drain* = Storm season arrives, demand increases, turbine efficiency drops. The longer you run, the harder conditions get — a completely natural physical escalation.
- *Transfer at cost* = Shunting power between systems loses energy to heat and resistance. You can push battery charge into the grid, but you lose 15% in transmission. Every transfer is a real sacrifice.
- *Completing orders* = The coastal shipping network sends supply runs in exchange for power contracts. You fulfill a "power contract" (divert X units of output for Y minutes) and receive a shipment of fuel or a maintenance crew that boosts efficiency. The orders are the supply chain.
- *Survival arc* = You're keeping the town alive. Failure means blackout — the fiction makes loss feel weighty without being dramatic.

**Sensory Identity:** Muted industrial blues and grays with amber warning lights. The visual palette is weathered metal panels, blinking indicator dials, and rain-streaked glass. Sound design: constant low turbine hum, rising in pitch as systems stress; wind and wave ambience; crackling radio communications when orders arrive. Music: slow, atmospheric drone that grows more dissonant as drain rates increase. Screen feel: deliberate, slightly heavy — every action has a satisfying *clunk*.

**UI/UX Hooks:** The three pools are physical gauges — analog needle meters mounted on a control panel, not health bars. The "transfer" action is a physical valve or switch the player flips, with visible pipes connecting subsystems. Orders arrive as radio dispatches with a brief audio crackle. The HUD *is* the control room. No menus — it's all dials and switches.

**Narrative Hooks:**
- The accelerating drain is explained by storm season worsening — each "era" of difficulty is a new week of deteriorating weather, tied to a calendar the player can see.
- Failure means the town goes dark. The last thing that happens before game over is the lights flickering out one by one on the town silhouette in the background — visible from the station window.
- Mastery means the town survives a legendary storm that has historically destroyed every previous keeper. Local lore about past keepers who failed is ambient flavor text on order cards.

**Fusion Rating: ★★★★★**
Every mechanic is a natural physical law in this setting. Drain, transfer loss, and supply contracts are all things real power infrastructure does. The three-pool structure matches real generator/storage/distribution topology. The escalating difficulty is just weather.

### 2. The Apothecary's Equilibrium

**Elevator Pitch:** You run an alchemical apothecary in a fantasy city, keeping three volatile reagent vats — Fire Essence, Verdant Extract, and Void Compound — from destabilizing while fulfilling the city's potion orders.

**Mechanical Mapping:**
- *Three draining pools* = Three reagent vats that slowly evaporate or decay at different rates. Fire Essence burns off fastest. Void Compound destabilizes slowly but catastrophically when depleted.
- *Accelerating drain* = The city is under increasing magical stress (a looming catastrophe, a festival, a war). Demand and reagent volatility both increase over time.
- *Transfer at cost* = You can convert reagents using transmutation, but the process wastes a portion — it's imprecise and costs raw material.
- *Completing orders* = City guilds send orders for potions. Fulfill them by committing the right reagents; receive gold to buy new stock from suppliers. Orders are the income stream.
- *Survival arc* = You're trying to keep the apothecary functioning through a crisis. Depletion of any vat means you can no longer produce key potions, triggering collapse.

**Sensory Identity:** Warm amber and deep purple. Bubbling vats with animated particle effects — gently simmering when healthy, boiling and sparking when low. Sound: alchemical bubbling, glass clinking, distant city sounds. Music: baroque harpsichord with a slight unhinged quality, growing more frantic. The visual style is richly detailed 2D — stained wood counters, labeled jars, copper tubing connecting the vats.

**UI/UX Hooks:** The three pools are literal alchemical vats on screen — cylindrical glass containers with colored liquid at varying fill levels. The liquid visibly churns and changes color as it approaches depletion. Orders arrive as rolled parchment scrolls dropping onto a counter. Transfer is represented as tilting one vat to pour into another through a tube — with visible loss as vapor escaping.

**Narrative Hooks:**
- The accelerating drain is tied to a magical event that the player cannot stop — only survive. Each difficulty threshold is a new phase of the event, with ambient dialogue from customers who grow increasingly desperate.
- Failure means the apothecary's reagents go critical — an explosion, a magical implosion, or simply the reagents turn inert. The city suffers for lack of medicines.
- Player mastery represents the apothecary becoming legendary — orders from increasingly important clients (the king, the archmage) as a reward for surviving longer.

**Fusion Rating: ★★★★★**
Reagents evaporating, transmutation waste, and potion orders are all deeply natural alchemy fictions. The three-pool structure maps perfectly to three distinct magical substances. The urgency escalation fits a magical crisis narrative.

### 3. Watershed

**Elevator Pitch:** You are a water rights manager in an arid region, balancing three river tributaries — their flows, reservoir levels, and irrigation demand — to keep farms, the town, and wildlife all above survival thresholds.

**Mechanical Mapping:**
- *Three draining pools* = Three water allocations: the upstream reservoir (fills slowly, depletes as you draw), the town's cistern (constant demand from population), and the irrigation channel (farms draw continuously during growing season).
- *Accelerating drain* = Drought deepens over the season. Evaporation increases. Upstream flow slows. The dry season is a natural, visible escalation.
- *Transfer at cost* = Diverting water between systems requires pumping — it costs energy (a secondary resource) and loses water to pipe leakage and evaporation. You cannot move water without losing some.
- *Completing orders* = Outside contractors offer water-access deals: let a mining company draw X units and they'll fund pipe repairs (reducing transfer loss) or a new pump (increasing throughput). Orders are agreements with external parties.
- *Survival arc* = Keeping the land alive through the drought. Failure is desertification — the farms die, the town evacuates, the ecosystem collapses. A slow, sad ending.

**Sensory Identity:** Warm ochres, cracked earth tones, and the blue-green of water standing out against the dryness. Visually minimal — a map view of the watershed with animated water flows. Sound: trickling water (healthy), dry wind (depleted), distant bird calls. Music: sparse acoustic guitar, unhurried, melancholic. The feel is contemplative and documentary-like.

**UI/UX Hooks:** The map view is the UI — pools are visible reservoirs and cisterns on a landscape. Pool levels are waterline heights in illustrated containers. Orders arrive as letters from contractors — formal, bureaucratic documents that feel weighty. Transfers are represented as opening valves on a pipeline schematic.

**Narrative Hooks:**
- The drought is a known event — the player starts with a weather almanac predicting when it will get worse. They can see the escalation coming but cannot stop it.
- Failure is gradual: farms go brown before they die. The town's ambient sounds fade. Wildlife disappears from the edges of the map. The game communicates loss through environmental decay rather than a game-over screen.
- Mastery represents institutional knowledge — the player becomes the person who figured out how to keep this region alive, and their decisions are preserved as a kind of community memory.

**Fusion Rating: ★★★★**
Water management maps excellently to pool mechanics — draining, transferring at loss, and contracts are all real hydrology/resource management concepts. Slight deduction because "completing orders" as contractor deals requires a small conceptual leap from the core water-management fiction.

### 4. Neural Cascade

**Elevator Pitch:** You are a neurotechnology operator managing three neural substrates in a synthetic mind — dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine — keeping a patient's neurochemistry stable while their condition deteriorates.

**Mechanical Mapping:**
- *Three draining pools* = Three neurotransmitter systems that naturally deplete through normal neural activity. Each has a different baseline drain rate and different consequences for depletion (dopamine loss = anhedonia, serotonin loss = anxiety, norepinephrine loss = fatigue).
- *Accelerating drain* = The patient's condition worsens over time — the underlying pathology increases demand on all systems simultaneously. This is clinical realism.
- *Transfer at cost* = Neurochemical rebalancing is imprecise — pushing one transmitter up by stimulating its precursors also draws from shared biochemical pathways, reducing another. Every intervention has side effects.
- *Completing orders* = Therapeutic tasks assigned by the medical team. Achieve target neurochemical windows for specific durations and receive new pharmacological tools or dosing permissions. Orders are treatment protocols.
- *Survival arc* = Keeping the patient functional — not cured, but stable. Failure is a crisis event: seizure, catatonia, acute episode.

**Sensory Identity:** Clean, clinical whites with deep blues and greens. Bioluminescent neural network visualizations — nodes firing across a dark background. The aesthetic is somewhere between a medical instrument and a screensaver. Sound: soft electronic tones, rhythmic heartbeat, occasional alert chimes. Music: ambient electronic, minimal, with binaural undertones. The feel is precise and tense.

**UI/UX Hooks:** The three pools are waveform displays — EEG-like readouts showing neurochemical levels over time. Pool depletion is a flatline or extreme spike on the readout. Transfer actions are drug-dispensing controls — you dial a dosage and commit. Orders arrive as printed protocol sheets from a printer visible on-screen. The interface feels like ICU monitoring equipment.

**Narrative Hooks:**
- The escalating drain tells the story of a degenerative condition — the patient was stable, then deteriorating, then in crisis. The game's difficulty arc is the disease progression.
- Failure is meaningful: a clinical incident that the player can review. The game doesn't say "you lose" — it says "session ended, review your interventions."
- Mastery represents an operator who can hold a patient at the edge of stability, achieving things the standard protocols say are impossible — a kind of clinical artistry.

**Fusion Rating: ★★★★**
Neurochemistry maps tightly to draining, interconnected resource pools. Transfer cost as biochemical side effects is elegant. Slight friction: "orders" as treatment protocols requires the player to feel like a medical operator rather than a passive caretaker, which might be alienating if not framed carefully.

### 5. The Candle Archive

**Elevator Pitch:** You are the last archivist of a vast underground library, managing three great candelabras that light the reading halls — keeping the flames alive while scholars bring you wax, wicks, and requests.

**Mechanical Mapping:**
- *Three draining pools* = Three candelabra banks: the East Wing (philosophical texts), the West Wing (scientific manuscripts), and the Vault (restricted archives). Each burns at a different rate depending on how many scholars are working.
- *Accelerating drain* = The library is increasingly crowded as the outside world deteriorates — more refugees, more scholars, more demand for light. The candles burn faster as the halls fill up.
- *Transfer at cost* = You can move candles between wings, but transport always costs flame — candles tip, drip, extinguish during movement. You lose some light in every transfer.
- *Completing orders* = Scholars bring wax and wick donations in exchange for access to specific sections. Fulfill their requests (allow access, retrieve specific volumes) and they pay in materials that let you restock. Orders are patron relationships.
- *Survival arc* = Keeping knowledge accessible during a slow civilizational crisis. Failure means the archives go dark — the knowledge is still there, but inaccessible.

**Sensory Identity:** Deep amber and shadow. The visual palette is pools of warm candlelight against stone darkness. The game looks like a Vermeer painting — intimate, warm, precise. Sound: candle crackle, parchment rustling, distant echoing footsteps, muffled voices. Music: solo cello or lute, quiet and unhurried. The feel is melancholic but cozy — a game about holding back darkness.

**UI/UX Hooks:** The three pools are visualized as candelabra — the flame count visible on each, flickering and diminishing. Pool levels are literal flame heights. Transfer is drag-and-drop candlesticks between visualized wings. Orders arrive as scholar request cards — handwritten, illustrated, with personality. The UI is a beautifully rendered archive map.

**Narrative Hooks:**
- The accelerating drain tells the story of civilizational collapse outside — each difficulty escalation is the outside world getting worse, driving more people into the archive.
- Failure is the archive going dark: the last candle gutters out. The scholars leave. The knowledge remains, unread, in the dark. It's a haunting game-over image.
- Mastery means the archivist becomes a legendary figure — the one who kept the Archive of the Long Night burning through everything. Scholar characters remember your actions.

**Fusion Rating: ★★★★★**
This is arguably the purest mechanical-thematic fusion: candles *literally* drain over time, transfer *literally* costs flame, and patron orders *literally* fund the operation with materials. Every mechanic is also a concrete physical action in the fiction. The three-pool structure maps to three wings of a library, which is believable architecture.

### 6. Estuary Protocol

**Elevator Pitch:** You manage a wetland preserve — three interconnected ecosystems (freshwater marsh, salt flat, and estuary delta) — maintaining ecological balance while a development company encroaches and climate pressures mount.

**Mechanical Mapping:**
- *Three draining pools* = Three ecosystem health meters: biodiversity index, water quality (salinity balance), and vegetation coverage. Each depletes as natural stressors (drought, salinity intrusion, runoff) operate continuously.
- *Accelerating drain* = Climate conditions worsen over time — more extreme weather events, faster salinity intrusion, longer dry periods. The game's difficulty escalation is climate change, which is real, visible, and emotionally resonant.
- *Transfer at cost* = Diverting resources between ecosystems (water, nutrients, species seeding) disrupts what you're pulling from. Introducing freshwater to the salt flat helps salinity but stresses salt-adapted species.
- *Completing orders* = Environmental grants and partnerships. Fulfill a research team's survey requirements or a conservation organization's milestone, and receive funding or physical resources (new plant species, water treatment equipment).
- *Survival arc* = Keeping the wetland alive long enough for legal protection to take effect — a countdown to sanctuary status that keeps getting pushed back.

**Sensory Identity:** Rich greens and blues with golden light. Lush, semi-realistic 2D art — watercolor-inspired. Sound: birdsong, water movement, wind through reeds, distant construction machinery. Music: ambient folk with field recordings embedded in the mix. The feel is both beautiful and quietly sad — a game about something worth saving.

**UI/UX Hooks:** A top-down illustrated map is the primary view. Ecosystem pools are visualized as color saturation and density changes to the terrain — a healthy marsh is vivid green, a depleted one goes brown and bare. Orders arrive as grant applications and field reports — bureaucratic documents with a slightly absurdist tone given the urgency. Transfers are represented as ecological intervention tools (a pump, a seed spreader, a filtration system).

**Narrative Hooks:**
- The accelerating drain tells a climate story — the player isn't fighting a villain, they're fighting physics. The deterioration has causes the game acknowledges but the player can't reverse.
- Failure means the wetland is sold to developers. The game-over sequence shows construction machinery moving in — a slow, sad transition from living map to gray pavement.
- Mastery means the preserve survives long enough to become protected — a bittersweet victory. The land is safe, but scarred. The game acknowledges that "winning" doesn't mean "healed."

**Fusion Rating: ★★★★**
Ecosystem management maps naturally to parallel draining pools — environmental systems genuinely work this way. Excellent thematic resonance. Slight friction: "completing orders" as grant applications has a slight administrative mismatch with the urgency of the gameplay.

### 7. The Three Cisterns of Ur-Kesh

**Elevator Pitch:** You are a water priest in an ancient Mesopotamian city-state, maintaining the sacred cisterns that provide life to three districts — temple, market, and fields — while the dry season approaches and political demands multiply.

**Mechanical Mapping:**
- *Three draining pools* = Three cisterns: the Temple Cistern (ritual purity water, drains slowest but cannot be allowed to drop — sacrilege), Market Cistern (commerce and cooling, drains at medium rate), and Field Channels (agricultural supply, drains fastest in summer).
- *Accelerating drain* = Seasonal escalation — the dry season arrives in phases. Solstice brings peak drain rates. You know it's coming but can't stop the calendar.
- *Transfer at cost* = Moving water between cisterns requires servants and vessels — it's labor-intensive and loses water to spill and evaporation. Political cost too: diverting from the Temple is scandalous.
- *Completing orders* = Petitions from the three districts. The temple wants purity water for rituals; merchants want water for trade goods; farmers need irrigation. Fulfill petitions using cistern water, and receive gold, grain, or political favor — which you use to buy water from traders.
- *Survival arc* = Keeping the city-state alive through the dry season, pleasing the three power centers without letting any cistern run dry (dry Temple = religious crisis; dry Market = economic crisis; dry Fields = famine).

**Sensory Identity:** Warm ochres and sun-bleached whites with lapis lazuli blue for water. Clay tablet aesthetic — UI elements look like cuneiform inscriptions and painted pottery. Sound: wind across desert sand, water pouring, market chatter in the distance, ceremonial drums. Music: ancient modal scales on reed instruments, slow and ceremonial. The feel is stately and ancient.

**UI/UX Hooks:** The cisterns are drawn as architectural cross-sections — you can see the waterline dropping in stone-cut containers. Petitions arrive as clay tablets with illustrated requests. Transfer is represented as a canal map — you redirect flow by adjusting the illustrated water routing. The "gold/grain/favor" economy is tracked on a small accounting tablet in the corner.

**Narrative Hooks:**
- The escalating drain is the seasonal calendar — the player can see the months passing, the solstice approaching. The game's difficulty arc is also a cultural calendar full of events and obligations.
- Failure means the city fails: the wrong cistern drying triggers an appropriate crisis (religious, economic, or famine), each with a different ending. Multiple failure modes give replay texture.
- Mastery represents a legendary Water Priest who kept Ur-Kesh alive through the great drought — historical legacy as a game mechanic, with previous runs commemorated in the city's oral history.

**Fusion Rating: ★★★★★**
Ancient water management is perhaps the most historically literal interpretation of this mechanic set — Mesopotamian civilizations genuinely survived on cistern management and water priest institutions. Every mechanic is what a water priest actually did. The three-district structure gives natural thematic differentiation to the three pools.

## Recommendations

**Ranked by fusion quality and overall design potential:**

**Tier 1 — Highest Fusion (build any of these):**

1. **The Candle Archive** (★★★★★) — The strongest overall recommendation for an indie/browser game. The mechanics are *literally* the fiction: candles drain, transfers cost flame, patrons bring wax. The aesthetic is immediately distinctive (Vermeer-dark archive) and highly achievable with stylized 2D art. The emotional tone — quiet, melancholic, cozy resistance against darkness — is rare in the strategy game space and will stand out. Small scope feels *complete*. The fail state is beautiful and haunting. **Best for a solo dev or tiny team.**

2. **Tidal Station** (★★★★★) — Best if you want an industrial/mechanical aesthetic and a more "serious" tone. The control-panel UI concept (dials, switches, analog gauges) is highly distinctive and offers strong diegetic UI potential. The setting is grounded, believable, and emotionally resonant without being heavy. The power/grid/battery topology is technically accurate. **Best for players who respond to systems and infrastructure.**

3. **The Three Cisterns of Ur-Kesh** (★★★★★) — Best for historical/cultural distinctiveness. No other browser strategy game is set in ancient Mesopotamia with water-priest mechanics. The three-pool differentiation (Temple/Market/Fields = different political consequences for depletion) adds narrative texture that the other settings don't automatically provide. Multiple failure modes give replay variety. **Best for narrative depth and market differentiation.**

**Tier 2 — Strong Fusion (excellent alternatives):**

4. **The Apothecary's Equilibrium** (★★★★★) — Very strong, but the fantasy alchemist setting is more crowded market territory. If your game already has a fantasy art direction, this is the natural fit. The reagent vat visualization is instantly readable and satisfying. **Best if fantasy is already your direction.**

5. **Tidal Station** and **The Apothecary** are interchangeable in quality — the choice between them is purely about which sensory identity fits your art capabilities and target audience.

6. **Neural Cascade** (★★★★) — The most unusual and potentially most memorable setting if executed well. The neurochemistry framing makes the three pools feel genuinely different from each other (each has a distinct *consequence* for depletion). Risk: the clinical setting might feel cold. Reward: nothing else on Steam looks like this. **Best for a developer comfortable with unusual, niche appeal.**

7. **Watershed** (★★★★) — Contemplative and documentary-feeling. Strong for a certain audience but may be too quiet for idle-ish gameplay expectations. The environmental storytelling is exceptional. **Best for environmental/documentary aesthetic games.**

8. **Estuary Protocol** (★★★★) — Thematically rich, emotionally resonant, climate-conscious. The grant application as "orders" is slightly awkward mechanically. But the visual style (watercolor wetlands) is achievable and beautiful. **Best for a politically/environmentally engaged audience.**

---

**Indie feasibility notes:**

- **The Candle Archive** wins the art pipeline assessment: the aesthetic is achievable with hand-drawn or AI-assisted 2D art, limited animation, and a small asset set. Dark backgrounds with warm light are technically forgiving — you can make something beautiful without a large art team.
- **Tidal Station** wins for UI/UX distinctiveness — the analog control panel concept is a strong visual hook for Steam capsule art and trailers.
- **Ur-Kesh** wins for market differentiation — there is genuinely no browser strategy game in ancient Mesopotamia. The niche is open.
- All settings support a scope that feels *intentionally* small — none imply a vast open world that the game can't deliver.
