# Setting Design: Corporate Deckbuilder

## The Core Problem

The risk with a corporate career deckbuilder is that "office politics" as a theme almost automatically reads as satire. The moment you name a card "Synergize Stakeholders" or show a boss with a health bar labeled "Q3 Review," players are in comedy mode — laughing at the absurdity rather than feeling the actual stakes. For the metaphor to land as something real, the game needs to make players feel the specific texture of corporate life: the ambiguity, the patience required, the way relationships and reputation actually work, the genuine fear of being found out or left behind.

The setting choice is the lever that controls this. Satire happens when the corporate world is viewed from outside, at ironic distance. Immersion happens when the player is inside a specific, textured version of that world with something real to lose.

---

## Recommended Setting: Mid-Level at a Consultancy, Late 1990s

**Time:** 1997–2001. The late boom years before the crash.

**Place:** A mid-sized management consultancy — not McKinsey, not a startup. The kind of firm that exists in every city: regional offices, a mix of Fortune 500 and struggling mid-market clients, partners who made their money in the 1980s and associates who are betting their 20s on making partner before 35.

**Player character:** You are an Associate, three years in. Not a newcomer. Not yet senior. You know enough to understand what's happening around you, not enough to control it.

### Why This Setting Works

**The era does specific work.** 1997–2001 is the last moment before email became the primary medium of corporate communication, before everything was logged and discoverable. Conversations happened in hallways and on planes and over dinners. Influence was genuinely relational rather than algorithmic. This means your "talking points" and "professional tactics" have a context where they feel true — you really were building a deck of verbal and relational tools, because that was the technology of advancement. A card called "Read the Room" in a 2024 setting feels like a joke. In 1997, walking into a senior partner's office and correctly reading whether he'd had a good or bad flight was a genuine skill that could make or break a relationship.

**Consulting makes the mechanics feel earned.** Consultancies are one of the few corporate environments where the work is literally about persuasion and framing. You are paid to walk into a room, understand what's happening, and reshape how people think about a problem. The "talking points" aren't a metaphor stretched over a different activity — they are the activity. A deck of communication and analytical tools is exactly what a consultant builds over a career. Players won't feel like they're playing a game about work; they'll feel like they're doing the work.

**The stakes are legible without exaggeration.** Getting fired from a consultancy in 1997 means: your visa status may be in question (if you're on an H-1B, which many associates were), your peer group moves on without you, the firm has institutional memory of why you left, and the consulting world is small enough that the story follows you. Getting promoted means: you clear a bar most people don't clear, you get significantly more money, and you enter a different social world. Neither outcome needs to be dramatized. Players who understand the context will feel both ends of it.

**No smartphones means no escape.** There's a specific texture to professional anxiety before constant connectivity: you couldn't check your standing in real time. You had to wait to find out where you stood. The game can reproduce this — outcomes of encounters resolve on a delay, you don't know how a meeting went until you hear secondhand, reputation shifts happen offscreen and are reported back to you. This isn't a design gimmick; it's accurate to the period and it creates genuine tension.

## The Three Boss Encounters, Grounded

### 1. The Performance Review

In a consultancy, this isn't a single annual event — it's a cascade. Peer evaluations feed into a partner evaluation, which feeds into a committee discussion you're not in the room for. By the time you hear the outcome, a dozen conversations have already happened.

The encounter design should reflect this. The "boss" isn't a single partner — it's the evaluation system itself, which means the things you've done and said across the run are being weighed by people with different memories and agendas. Cards you played three encounters ago show up here as consequences. Tactics that impressed one stakeholder may have read differently to another.

What makes this feel real rather than satirical: the outcome isn't about whether you performed well objectively. It's about whether the right people have the right story about you. A player who did excellent analytical work but failed to make it visible to the partners who control the committee will fare worse than one who did adequate work but managed relationships well. This is not cynical — it's accurate. The game should present this neutrally, not as corruption but as the actual system.

### 2. The Hostile Takeover / Client Crisis

A more accurate framing for this encounter: the client has decided to pull the engagement, and you've been sent in to save it. You're the third person on this project. The original partner is unavailable. The client's CFO is hostile because the first two months delivered nothing they could use.

The "hostility" isn't cartoonish villain energy — it's institutional frustration that is completely legitimate. The client is right to be angry. Your job is to demonstrate enough competence and enough understanding of their actual problem (not the problem the firm told them they had) to buy another quarter.

Cards here might include: acknowledging what went wrong (difficult — costs short-term credibility, builds long-term trust), reframing the deliverables around what the client actually cares about, finding a quick win that changes the emotional temperature of the room. The encounter rewards players who have built a deck capable of genuine diagnosis rather than pure persuasion.

### 3. Office Politics

The most easily satirized encounter, and the one that most needs careful handling.

The real version of this encounter: someone who was supposed to be a peer has been quietly building a case that you're not adding value. They haven't done this maliciously — they're also trying to survive and advance. They've simply described situations in ways that serve their narrative, and over time that narrative has accumulated weight.

You find out about this not through a confrontation but through a small signal: a partner mentions something in passing that could only have come from one source. You now have to decide what to do with that information.

The encounter isn't about defeating an opponent. It's about whether you have the relational capital to correct a narrative without looking defensive, whether you can demonstrate value directly rather than through comparison, and whether you're willing to have a direct conversation with the colleague — which may preserve the relationship or may not, but either way resolves the ambiguity.

Cards here: "Seek Clarification" (high risk, high resolution), "Demonstrate Directly" (bypasses the conflict, slower), "Find a Sponsor" (requires relationship cards already in hand), "Let It Lie" (loses points with the partner, preserves the relationship). None of these are clearly correct. The encounter should leave players thinking about what they would actually do.

## Deck Architecture, Grounded in the Setting

**Talking Points** should reflect the actual vocabulary of consulting: not just buzzwords, but genuine analytical moves. Cards like:

- "Issue Tree" — restructure a problem into components, reducing cognitive load for the client, gaining clarity on where the real problem lives
- "So What Test" — challenge a finding: does this piece of analysis change what we do? Prunes weak cards from the deck over time as a passive effect
- "Pyramid Principle" — lead with the recommendation, support with evidence; doubles persuasion effectiveness but reduces the appearance of humility
- "Hypothesis-Driven" — commit to a direction early, gain speed, risk being wrong in a visible way

These aren't satirical card names. They're real concepts from the actual methodology of management consulting in this era. A player who learns what these terms mean while playing the game is learning something true about how consultants are trained to think.

**Professional Tactics** should cover relational and reputational moves:

- "Manage Up" — invest time with a senior partner, builds a relationship card but costs time on client work
- "Develop a Point of View" — form and share a perspective on something beyond your current project; builds reputation, creates vulnerability to being wrong
- "Protect Your Air Cover" — ensure your sponsor in the firm knows what you're doing and why; costs a card slot, provides insurance
- "Find the Real Decision-Maker" — in any client engagement, the org chart often doesn't reflect where the actual decision lives; identifying the right person reshapes what cards are useful

**The Run Arc**

Starting deck: heavy on analytical tools, light on relational ones. Early encounters reward correctness and rigor. As the run progresses, encounters increasingly require relational capital you may not have built. Players who stayed purely analytical hit a ceiling. Players who over-invested in relationships may lack the credibility that comes from demonstrated work quality.

This arc is true to the actual trajectory of a consulting career. Junior people are evaluated on technical skill. Senior people are evaluated on client relationship and business development. The transition between these two modes is where most people stall or wash out. The game can make players feel that transition rather than just describing it.

## Tone and Presentation

**Prose tone:** Flat, precise, slightly exhausted. The internal monologue of someone who has been working 70-hour weeks for three years and is still, genuinely, interested in the work. Not cynical. Not cheerful. Competent and a little worn.

**Visual reference:** Not the garish palette of a corporate satire. Think: the beige and navy of a late-90s office building, fluorescent light on carpet tile, the specific off-white of a Kinko's printout. Readable data tables. Helvetica. The color of a manila folder.

**What the game never does:** It never winks at the player about how absurd corporate life is. The encounters are taken seriously. The stakes are presented as real. Players who find the setting absurd are welcome to, but the game itself is not in on the joke. This is the crucial distinction between satire and immersion — the game treats the world it depicts as a world where things genuinely matter to the people in it.

---

## Why This Works for the Mechanics

The Slay the Spire structure maps cleanly because:

- **Deck thinning and optimization** corresponds to the real process of figuring out which of your tools are actually useful versus which ones you carry out of habit or training
- **Boss encounters with specific mechanics** corresponds to the real experience of each high-stakes moment having its own particular shape — a performance review is not like a client crisis is not like a political situation
- **Run permadeath** corresponds to the career reality that some things can't be recovered from; being fired or promoted out genuinely ends one chapter and begins another
- **Card synergies** corresponds to the real phenomenon where certain combinations of skills and relationships unlock possibilities that neither alone would

The setting doesn't force the mechanics into a metaphor. It finds the mechanics already present in the subject matter and names them clearly.
