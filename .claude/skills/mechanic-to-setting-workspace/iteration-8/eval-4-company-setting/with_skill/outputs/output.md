## The Feel of This Game

You sit down. Three sources in front of you, each slightly different, each unpredictable. You spend what you have to pull something out — maybe useful, maybe not. Your workspace is small and filling up fast. You combine what you can, discard what you must, and try to match what's being asked of you with what you happened to get. The rhythm is: spend → receive → assess → arrange → deliver → spend again. It's a cycle of converting uncertainty into adequacy. Not mastery — adequacy. You're never in full control. You develop intuitions. You learn when to push your luck and when to cut your losses. Sometimes the system hands you exactly what you need and it feels like grace. Sometimes it hands you garbage five times in a row and you make it work anyway.

The emotional core: navigating a world that won't tell you what's coming, with just enough agency to feel responsible for the outcome.

## Theme

How humans coexist with uncertainty — not as a philosophical abstraction, but as the texture of daily life. The specific register here is workplace uncertainty: you show up, you do what's in front of you, the rules keep subtly shifting, and you develop a relationship with the chaos. Some days it feels like freedom. Some days it feels like drowning. Most days it feels like both.

## Setting Concept: 万象回收站 (Wànxiàng Huíshōu Zhàn) — The Kaleidoscope Reclamation Office

**Elevator Pitch**

You work the intake desk at a company that reclaims objects from futures that didn't happen — sorting, grading, and fulfilling salvage orders for clients who need pieces of timelines that were never built.

**Why This Fits**

The game's core is pulling unknown items from categorized-but-unpredictable sources and assembling them to meet external demands. A company that recovers objects from unrealized futures makes this loop feel inevitable rather than arbitrary. The pools aren't random because the game needs randomness — they're random because that's literally what unresolved futures produce. You don't know what you'll get because *the timeline those objects came from never stabilized enough to be predictable*. The backpack limit isn't a game constraint — it's a containment protocol, because unresolved-future objects degrade if too many occupy the same present-tense space. Merging identical items into higher grades isn't an abstract upgrade mechanic — it's temporal consolidation, the standard industry process for stabilizing fragments from the same probability branch.

And the theme lives in the premise without ever being stated: you are literally a person whose entire job is coexisting with uncertainty. The futures these objects come from didn't happen. They're not real. But the objects are here, they're tangible, and someone is paying for them. Every day you show up and make something useful out of what-might-have-been. That's the game. That's also the theme.

**The Business — Designer's Full Answer**

The company is called 万象回收 (Wànxiàng Huíshōu), officially registered as a "speculative materials reclamation service." It was founded in 2019.

Here's the reality the designer knows: Probability branches — possible futures — don't cleanly disappear when they fail to actualize. They leave residue. Physical objects that "almost existed" sometimes precipitate into the present, the way moisture condenses on a cold surface. This has always happened — it's why people occasionally find objects they don't remember buying, why warehouses sometimes contain inventory that was never ordered. For most of history this was background noise. But starting around 2016, the rate accelerated dramatically. Nobody knows exactly why (though the company's founder has a theory — see The Mystery below). The volume of precipitated objects became large enough to be an industrial problem.

万象回收 exists because someone figured out how to *harvest* this precipitation systematically rather than just cleaning it up. The founder, 周若水 (Zhōu Ruòshuǐ), a former insurance actuary, developed the Extraction Apparatus (机器) — a device that doesn't create objects but acts as a *condensation point*, drawing precipitated matter from specific probability categories and materializing it in a controlled environment. The machine is categorized into channels (the pools) because probability residue clusters by domain — domestic objects, medical supplies, office materials, kitchen implements, electronics. You can tune the machine to a domain, but you can't control what specific object or what grade of temporal stability (rarity) comes through.

Who pays: Clients are a mix of insurance companies settling claims on objects that "should have existed" in covered scenarios, government agencies managing timeline-residue contamination, academic institutions studying probability artifacts, and — increasingly — private collectors who want objects from specific unrealized futures for reasons they don't disclose. The orders are real contracts with real deadlines. The company is profitable. It has a healthcare plan.

**A Day in This World**

You badge in at 8:40. Your station is one of twelve in the extraction bay — a long room with the Apparatus running down the center like a spine, each station a windowed alcove with its own extraction port. The machine hums at a frequency you've stopped noticing. Three channel lights are green today: 药品 (pharmaceuticals), 文具 (stationery), 电器 (electronics). The other two channels are dark — cycling, according to the morning memo, though nobody explains what cycling means.

Each channel has a status tag clipped to its port. Today 药品 reads "提纯" — purified. You know from experience this means the channel is pulling from a narrow probability band: everything that comes through will be at least Rare-grade stability. 文具 reads "波动" — fluctuating. Either perfectly stable mundane objects or near-mythical artifacts, nothing in between. 电器 has no tag. Standard extraction.

You check your order queue on the terminal. Three standing orders, one priority. The priority is from a government client: they need a Rare-grade pharmaceutical item, any type, by end of shift. The standing orders are more specific — an Epic-grade pen (stationery), two Excellent-grade kitchen items (but kitchen isn't even active today, so those will have to wait or you'll need to request a channel swap, which costs), and a matched set of common electronics.

Your containment tray has ten slots. This is non-negotiable — temporal containment fields can only stabilize ten discrete objects per operator at a time. You currently have six items from yesterday: two common aspirin bottles (same type, same grade — you could consolidate them into one Excellent-grade bottle, which frees a slot but commits the material), one Rare-grade fountain pen, an Epic-grade blender that you can't use for any current order, and two items you haven't identified yet because they precipitated late yesterday and you didn't have time to grade them.

First decision of the day: consolidate the aspirin bottles or keep them separate? Consolidated, the Excellent-grade bottle *almost* meets the government order's grade requirement, but not quite. Separate, they're useless individually but you could combine one with something new if you pull another common pharmaceutical today. The purified channel makes that unlikely — purified channels skip the common grades. You could pull from the standard electronics channel hoping for a tool drop (the machine occasionally produces reclamation tools alongside regular objects — a reforging kit that re-rolls stability grade, a transmutation prism that shifts an object's type within its domain, a star-blessing that boosts the next extraction). But that's a 20% chance and costs extraction credits you might need later.

You run the first extraction on the purified pharmaceutical channel. The port hisses. A small object materializes in the containment chamber: a bottle of eye drops, Rare-grade. You check the government order — they asked for Rare-grade pharmaceutical, any type. This qualifies. You could submit it now, clear the order, collect the payment. But: you have a standing skill from last quarter's evaluation — "强迫症," the matched-set bonus. If all items in a single order submission are from the same category, the payout doubles. The government order only requires one item, so matching is automatic. But if you hold and wait, hoping to combine this into an Epic and fill a *different* order later...

You submit the eye drops. The government order clears. 35 credits deposited.

The morning goes like this. You extract, you assess, you decide. Sometimes you extract and get something beautiful — a Legendary-grade mechanical pencil that seems to shimmer with the weight of a future where someone used it to draft something important. It doesn't fit any current order. You hold it because you can't bring yourself to recycle it, and now you have nine slots.

By lunch your tray is full and you still haven't cleared the pen order. You recycle the blender (it hurts; it was Epic-grade, but it's been sitting for two days and no kitchen orders have come through). You extract again. Common-grade stapler. The fluctuating stationery channel giving you the low end of its bimodal distribution. You extract again. Mythical-grade eraser. The high end. You didn't need an eraser. You needed a pen.

**Sensory Identity**

Visual: Clean, slightly over-lit institutional space — think a modern logistics center crossed with a hospital lab. Not grimy or dystopian. The aesthetic is *competent bureaucracy*. Color palette: warm whites, soft grays, muted teal for the containment fields, amber for extraction-in-progress, and brief flares of color when high-grade objects materialize (the rarity spectrum maps to increasingly saturated, warm-shifting hues). Objects themselves are rendered with a slight translucency at lower grades, becoming more opaque and present as stability increases — Mythical items look *more real than reality*, hyperdetailed and solid in a way that makes the rest of the screen feel slightly faded.

Sound: The machine's constant low hum (a drone in D, barely audible). Extraction sounds: a pressurization hiss, then a crystalline chime whose pitch rises with rarity — common is a dull click, Mythical is a clear bell that rings slightly too long. Consolidation makes a satisfying harmonic merge sound, two tones resolving into one. The order terminal chirps on completion. Ambient: distant footsteps, other operators' extractions muffled through walls, occasional PA announcements.

Music: Minimal ambient — long, patient synthesizer pads with very slow harmonic movement. Something between the Severance soundtrack and the Obra Dinn title theme. Not sad, not happy. Present.

**The World's Secret**

What the player notices first, gradually: the objects don't make sense.

Not in a dramatic way. In a quiet, nagging way. You pull a box of aspirin from the pharmaceutical channel, and the brand name is one letter off from a brand you know. The dosage is in a unit system you don't recognize. A pen comes through with a university logo — you look it up and the university doesn't exist, but the address is a real place; it's a parking lot. An electronic item has a power rating stamped in a standard that was proposed in 2014 but never adopted.

These aren't glitches. They're consistent. Every object tells a coherent story about a world that *almost* is yours but isn't. And the discrepancies aren't random — they cluster. Objects from the same extraction session often share the same "wrongness." A batch of stationery all bearing logos from companies that went bankrupt in your timeline but apparently thrived in theirs. A set of kitchen tools designed for a cuisine that doesn't exist, but whose ingredients are all real plants combined in ways no culture happened to try.

The mystery unfolds through the objects themselves — through their labels, their serial numbers, their wear patterns, their impossible details:

**Layer 1 (early game):** Small wrongnesses. Misspelled brands. Unfamiliar measurement units. The player might not even notice at first because the objects are functional and the orders don't care about labels.

**Layer 2 (mid game):** Patterns emerge. Objects extracted on the same day share consistent alternate histories. A Tuesday's haul all comes from a timeline where the 2011 earthquake didn't happen — products from Sendai manufacturers that in our world were destroyed, in packaging designed for a market that in our world contracted. A Thursday's objects all assume the existence of a technology (piezoelectric fabric) that in our world remained a lab curiosity.

**Layer 3 (late game):** The player finds objects that reference *万象回收 itself* — but wrong. A company manual for 万象回收 dated two years in the future, describing procedures that don't exist yet. An employee badge with the player's station number but a different name. An extraction log showing output volumes ten times higher than current capacity, dated six months from now. The company in the unrealized futures is *bigger*, and it's extracting from futures where it's bigger still, and those futures are extracting from futures where—

**The designer's full answer:** Zhou Ruoshui's theory is correct. The acceleration of probability precipitation isn't a natural phenomenon. It's a feedback loop. The Extraction Apparatus doesn't just passively collect precipitated objects — the act of extraction *destabilizes adjacent probability branches*, causing more precipitation, which enables more extraction. 万象回收 is profitable because its own operation generates its own supply. The company is, in a precise technical sense, *mining the futures where it doesn't exist to fuel the present where it does*. Every object the player extracts makes the futures those objects came from slightly less likely to actualize — and makes the future where 万象回收 keeps growing slightly more likely.

Zhou Ruoshui knows this. She considers it acceptable. The precipitated objects were never going to actualize anyway — she's just harvesting what would otherwise dissipate. But the objects from futures where the company is larger suggest that the feedback loop doesn't converge. It accelerates. And in the furthest-future objects the player finds, the ones with the highest temporal stability — Mythical-grade, pulled from the deepest unrealized branches — the world they describe is one where probability itself has become so unstable that *nothing is certain anymore*. Not in a catastrophic way. In an ambient way. A world where everyone lives the way the player already does at their extraction station: navigating uncertainty, making the best of what precipitates, never knowing what's coming next but showing up anyway.

The mystery's question, for the player: is the company *causing* the uncertainty of the future, or *preparing people to live in it*? Is this a cautionary tale or a training program? Zhou Ruoshui would say: both. The world is becoming uncertain whether 万象回收 exists or not. At least this way, someone's learning how to work with it.

**Mechanic Resonance**

- **Merging identical items into higher grades** feels like an act of commitment — you're collapsing two possibilities into one more stable reality. The moment of consolidation is the moment you stop hedging. In a game about uncertainty, the decision to *resolve* uncertainty (even partially) is the central dramatic act, and the merge mechanic makes you perform it dozens of times per session.

- **The fluctuating (波动) affix** — a channel that only produces Common or Legendary, nothing between — is the emotional thesis of the game compressed into a single pool. You can't hedge. You can't play it safe. You either get nothing useful or something extraordinary, and you have to decide *before* extracting whether you're willing to live with that. It's the setting's theme made into a button you press.

- **Full inventory with no matching orders** — the moment where your tray is full, nothing fits what's being asked of you, and you have to recycle something that cost real credits to extract. This is the feeling of sunk costs, of letting go of something you invested in because the world moved on. In this setting, you're literally discarding fragments of unrealized futures, and the act of recycling is an acknowledgment that not every possibility deserves to be held onto.

**Fusion Rating: ★★★★★**

The gameplay IS the life. Extracting from categorized-but-unpredictable sources, managing limited containment space, merging for stability, fulfilling external orders under pressure — every action the player takes is exactly what this character would do on a Tuesday. The theme — coexisting with uncertainty — isn't a message delivered through story beats; it's the texture of every decision. And the mystery lives in the objects themselves, the things the player is already handling, so it doesn't require a single extra system or side quest to deliver.

## Recommendation

This is the one. The premise is strange enough to hook ("you reclaim objects from futures that didn't happen"), the logic within it is airtight (the business model, the client base, the reason for the machine, the physics of probability precipitation), and the mystery grows organically from the daily work without requiring any narrative infrastructure beyond the objects the player is already examining. The theme doesn't need to be stated because the player is living it — every extraction is an act of coexisting with uncertainty, every merge is an act of resolving it, and every recycled item is an act of letting go.

The setting also solves a practical design problem: it gives the five item categories (fruit, medicine, stationery, kitchen, electronics) a reason to coexist that isn't "it's a convenience store." They coexist because probability precipitation doesn't respect market categories — unrealized futures contain everything.

## Next Steps

- Define the specific "wrongnesses" for each item pool. Pharmaceuticals might have unfamiliar dosage units; stationery might bear logos of nonexistent institutions; electronics might use standards that were proposed but never adopted. These details are the delivery mechanism for the mystery and should be visible in item tooltips or flavor text.
- Decide how much of the mystery is explicit (discoverable through UI elements like an "object report" screen) vs. ambient (just visible if you read the item descriptions closely). The best version probably layers both — early clues are ambient, later ones are impossible to miss.
- Zhou Ruoshui should be a presence in the game — not as a character you interact with, but as a voice in company memos, training materials, and policy updates. Her tone should be calm, competent, and slightly too comfortable with uncertainty. The player should gradually realize she knows more than the memos let on.
- The PA announcements and company communications are the primary narrative delivery channel. They should start mundane and become subtly stranger — not in a horror-escalation way, but in a "wait, did they just acknowledge something they shouldn't know?" way.
