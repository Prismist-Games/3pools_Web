# Principles (Detailed)

These expand on the essentials listed in SKILL.md. Read when you need deeper guidance on a
specific principle.

## Feel Over Function

Don't ask "what surface-level activity looks like this mechanic?" Ask "what life situation
*feels like* this gameplay?"

A game about repeatedly choosing from 3 uncertain sources isn't about "picking things from
three places" — it's about navigating uncertainty with limited information. A market trader, a
triage nurse, a refugee deciding which route to take — these feel right not because surface
actions match, but because the *emotional experience* matches.

## Internal Logic

Every setting must pass the "why?" test at every level. Why does this person do this work? Why
only one source at a time? Why can't they take everything?

If any answer is "because the game mechanic requires it," the setting is a skin, not a setting.
The setting should *generate* constraints naturally — mechanic limitations should feel like
consequences of the world.

The Internal Logic Test operates at two levels:

**Player level**: "If I were living in this world, would my daily actions naturally look like
the gameplay?"

**Designer level**: The designer needs solid *human logic* — motivations, stakes, relationships,
business viability. But the designer does NOT need to explain *metaphysical logic* — origins,
underlying physics, cosmological rules. If the setting has a strange element (a mysterious
machine, an inexplicable phenomenon), trying to explain why it exists triggers cascading
complexity. Know the rules of its *behavior*, not its *existence*.

Rigorous does not mean mundane. The premise can be strange or absurd. What must be rigorous is
the human logic within that premise.

## Spiral, Don't Map

The relationship between mechanics and setting is a spiral, not a checklist:

Feel/theme → find a resonant world → see which mechanics fit → notice which don't → ask
whether the mechanic should change or the setting should adapt → iterate.

Don't front-load a mapping table. Many mechanics evolve during development. What matters is
that the setting and core loop are deeply intertwined.

## Avoid the Obvious

Push beyond the first association. The most memorable indie games find unexpected thematic homes:
- Hades: roguelike death = literal escape from the underworld
- Papers, Please: document checking = moral experience
- Unpacking: inventory management = narrative device

## Respect the Scope

Indie games succeed by being focused. The setting should make a small game feel *intentionally*
small. A cozy shop is complete at small scale; a galactic empire is not.

## Consider the Fail State

What does failure *mean* in this setting? The best settings make failure feel thematic rather
than punitive.

## Sensory First, Lore Second

For light strategy games especially, the *feeling* of the setting matters more than
worldbuilding depth. Players should feel the setting through visuals, audio, and interaction
feedback — not through reading text boxes.

## Present, Don't Sell

No meta-commentary evaluating your own output. Don't write "this transforms a simple mechanic
into a profound statement about..." If the setting is good, the reader sees it. But "present,
don't sell" doesn't mean "make it boring." The premise itself should be compelling.

## Say What You Mean

Use plain, concrete language. If you write "fragments of someone's life" or "echoes of
possibility," ask: what does this actually mean in the world? If you can't replace it with a
concrete description, the concept isn't clear yet.

## Theme Is Non-Negotiable

When the user provides a theme, it's the single most important constraint. If a setting is
interesting but doesn't embody the theme, it fails. An interesting world that needs a character
to explain its theme has failed at theme integration. The theme must live in the systems, not
in the dialogue.

## Pillars Generate Questions, Not Answers

From Nordhagen's Jaws exercise: extract themes → frame each as a pillar → use pillars to ask
design questions → setting emerges from answering those questions.

A setting is good when it answers the design questions so naturally you forget they existed. If
you have to work around a question — "well, the player draws from three pools because..." — it's
the wrong setting. In the right setting, drawing from three pools is what anyone in this world
would obviously do.

Pillars are decision-making tools, not descriptions. "The sea is dangerous" is a description.
"Every interaction with the sea costs something" is a pillar — it tells you how to make design
decisions.

## Mechanics as Narrative Levers

From Nordhagen's *Conquering the Great White*: mechanics aren't just listed — they're shown as
levers that serve specific thematic pillars.

"Gold has no survival purpose for the player, but we want to include it to support our theme
of greed and the common good." Ask of every mechanic: which pillar does this serve? If none,
ask whether it *could*. A setting where every mechanic serves a pillar is stronger than one
where only the core loop does.

Don't create mapping tables. Instead, explain *why* the mechanic matters in narrative terms.
"The player merges items" is mechanical. "The player combines what they've gathered into
something worth more than its parts — the act of synthesis is the setting's central metaphor"
is a narrative lever.

If a mechanic can't serve any pillar, flag it — not as a failure, but as a question. "I don't
have to list any mechanics that aren't going to be used narratively — but if not, I should
think about if I could."

## Anticipate Dead Ends

From Nordhagen's *Where the Water Tastes Like Wine*: thematic fit does not equal good design.
Three common dead-end patterns:

1. **Tone mismatch** — The mechanic fits thematically but clashes with the game's tone. Mad
   Libs were thematically correct for a storytelling game but felt playful when the tone needed
   gravity.
2. **Playability conflict** — The mechanic is thematically perfect but miserable to play.
   Survival mechanics in a depression-era walking sim were on-theme but made the experience
   punishing rather than reflective.
3. **Logical but not interactive** — The element makes narrative sense but kills player agency.
   Intro folk stories were a logical framing device but weren't engaging to experience.

Surfacing these during concept phase is cheaper than discovering them in production. For each
setting concept, identify the most likely dead end so the team can stress-test early.

## No Right Answers

From Nordhagen on WTWTLW's walking speed: "I stand by it but I can also understand being
frustrated." On stories-as-currency: "that's of course not exactly what I wanted to say, but
I think it was the best way to translate storytelling into a gameplay verb."

Settings create tensions that won't fully resolve. Present these honestly. Rank concepts by
"most promising starting point," not "correct answer." Note what debates the team will face
and what tradeoffs each direction implies. The goal is informed decision-making, not false
certainty.
