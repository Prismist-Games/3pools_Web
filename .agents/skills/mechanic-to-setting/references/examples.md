# Examples

## Example 1: No theme provided

**Input:** "Turn-based tactics, small squad, permadeath for individual crew members, scavenge
resources between missions, meta-progression unlocks persist across runs."

**Output (abbreviated to 2 of 6 concepts):**

### The Feel of This Game
> This game feels like leading people you care about through danger, knowing you can't save
> everyone. The rhythm is: plan carefully → hold your breath → deal with what went wrong →
> scavenge what you can → plan again. The tension between tactical control and irreversible
> loss is the emotional core. Theme: preserving what matters under threat.

### Setting snippets

**Burned Archive**
> You lead scholars smuggling forbidden books out of a burning empire. Each scholar has unique
> knowledge — when one dies, what they carried is gone forever. Between missions, you search
> ruins for more texts.
>
> Why This Fits: Smuggling dangerous knowledge under threat is exactly this gameplay. Every
> mission is a choice about what's worth risking lives for. No explanation needed.
>
> Sensory Identity: Warm parchment tones and firelight. Charcoal and ash. Choral ambient music.

**Last Greenhouse**
> Botanists keeping seed vaults alive after ecological collapse. Each specialist grows strains
> no one else can.
>
> Why This Fits: Partial. "Nurturing life" fits the resource management, but "botanist"
> clashes with tactical combat. Needs reframing or the combat mechanics feel forced.
>
> Sensory Identity: Green against grey. Glass and condensation. Quiet electronic ambient.

### Theme Audit
> Burned Archive: "preserving knowledge under threat" emerges from gameplay — every mission
> is a choice about what's worth dying for. Passes.
>
> Last Greenhouse: "nurturing life in hostile conditions" is present but combat works against
> it. Conditional pass.

### Recommendations
> 1. Burned Archive — natural, vivid, expression: visual/environmental (low text dependency),
>    narrative depth: strong (why is the empire burning its knowledge?)
> 2. Last Greenhouse — expression: visual (low text), but mechanic awkwardness with combat.
>    Narrative depth: moderate.

## Example 2: Theme provided

**Input:** "3 resource pools that drain over time. Transfer between pools at a cost. Complete
orders to earn resources back. Drain accelerates. Browser idle-ish game. Theme: 'the quiet
anxiety of keeping things alive'."

**Key behaviors to notice:**

- Feel analysis leads with the theme, not the mechanics
- Theme audit honestly flags concepts where "quiet anxiety" doesn't come through (e.g., a
  setting that feels more heroic than anxious)
- Feasibility notes reflect browser/idle context (no 3D, no real-time action)
- At least one concept is unexpected — not the obvious "hospital" or "garden" association

## Example 3: New elements (verb inventory, design questions, narrative levers, implied systems)

**Input:** "Auction game. 3-5 players bid on items each round using a shared currency. Won items
go into your personal collection. Collections are scored at game end — sets and combos matter.
Each round, one player is the auctioneer and chooses what to sell. Digital tabletop, no
real-time elements."

**Output (abbreviated to 2 of 6 concepts):**

### The Feel of This Game
> This game feels like reading a room — watching what others want and deciding what you can
> afford to let go. The rhythm: evaluate → posture → commit → live with it. The tension lives
> in the gap between what something is worth to you and what you'll have to pay.
>
> **Tone:** Intimate, observational. A small room where everyone is watching everyone else.
> Silence before a bid matters more than the bid itself.
>
> **Thematic Pillars:** (1) Every transaction reveals something about you. (2) What you choose
> not to buy defines you as much as what you win.
>
> **Verb inventory:**
> - *Bid* — commitment under uncertainty; could carry weight of sacrifice, declaration, bluff
> - *Auction (as auctioneer)* — controlling what others must confront; power, curation, provocation
> - *Collect* — accumulation as identity; what you gather says who you are
> - *Pass/fold* — restraint as strategy; knowing when to let go
>
> **Constraints:** 3-5 players, turn-based, digital tabletop. No real-time pressure.
> Expressiveness must come through pacing and choice, not reflexes.

### Design Questions
> 1. Why do these people gather to trade *these* things?
> 2. Why does the auctioneer's choice of what to sell feel meaningful, not administrative?
> 3. What makes a collection more than the sum of its parts?
> 4. Why can't players just cooperate or trade directly?

### Setting snippets

**Memory Dealers**
> An underground market where people buy and sell recorded memories. Your collection is a
> curated identity — who you *choose* to have been.
>
> Why This Fits: Bidding on memories is bidding on identity — every purchase is a declaration
> of who you want to be. The auctioneer curates what the room must confront: a childhood, a
> crime, a first love. Passing on a memory means accepting you'll never have that experience.
> Sets matter because a coherent identity is worth more than fragments.
>
> Sensory Identity: Dim amber light. Muffled audio, like sounds through water. Each memory
> "plays" as a brief sensory flash when revealed — the smell of rain, a voice mid-sentence.
>
> Implied Systems: A "resonance" system where memories in your collection echo off each other —
> thematically linked memories unlock brief narrative vignettes between rounds.
>
> What Could Go Wrong: The premise is evocative but could feel too abstract — if memories are
> just cards with flavor text, the identity theme becomes a skin.

**Estate Dissolution**
> A family patriarch has died. The heirs gather to divide the estate. Every object has monetary
> value *and* sentimental value — and they diverge.
>
> Why This Fits: Bidding is family negotiation — you're spending shared inheritance to claim
> personal meaning. The auctioneer role becomes the executor choosing what to put on the table
> next, controlling the emotional temperature. Collections score because some objects only
> matter in context: a ring is jewelry, but a ring + a letter + a photograph is a love story.
>
> Sensory Identity: A wood-paneled study. Warm but tense. Objects photographed in clinical
> estate-sale lighting, then shown in warm memory-tinted flashbacks.
>
> Implied Systems: A "family tension" tracker that rises when bidding gets aggressive — at
> thresholds, family members make ultimatums or alliances shift.
>
> What Could Go Wrong: Sentimental value is hard to make mechanically legible without heavy
> text. Risk of "logical but not interactive" — backstory that explains value but doesn't
> create interesting decisions.

### Theme Audit
> Memory Dealers: theme comes through in the act of bidding — you're choosing an identity, not
> just winning an item. Passes mechanical storytelling (silent play still reads as "acquiring
> identity"). All verbs serve pillars. Strong pass.
>
> Estate Dissolution: theme present but relies more on text to convey sentimental value.
> Mechanical storytelling is weaker — without flavor text, it looks like a standard auction.
> Bid and collect serve pillars; auctioneer role is strong. Conditional pass.

### Recommendations
> 1. Memory Dealers — Speaks primarily through mechanical + environmental. Strong narrative
>    depth (what memories exist in this world? why are they traded?). Known tension: needs
>    the "memory flash" moments to land or it collapses into abstract card game.
> 2. Estate Dissolution — Speaks primarily through text + environmental. Known tension:
>    the team will debate how much narrative text is too much for a tabletop-paced game.

### Design Pillars (Memory Dealers)
> 1. **Every bid is a statement of identity.** If a purchase doesn't say something about who
>    the buyer wants to be, the item isn't designed well.
> 2. **Absence defines as much as presence.** What you don't have in your collection should
>    feel like a visible gap, not just a missed opportunity.
> 3. **The room is always watching.** Every mechanical action should feel observed — bids,
>    passes, and auctioneer choices all reveal information to other players.
> 4. **Memories are sensory, not textual.** Express memories through brief flashes of image,
>    sound, and color — not paragraphs of backstory.
